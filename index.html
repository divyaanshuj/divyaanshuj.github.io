About

I am a PhD Candidate in the Department of Economics at the University of California, Davis. My research is in Energy and Environmental Economics, with an emphasis on understanding the energy transition and its associated effects on wholesale power markets and health outcomes.

Projects In Progress

Storing the Sun: The Impacts of Renewable Energy on Wholesale Prices in the Era of Storage

California's wholesale electricity market has entered a new era in which grid-scale battery energy-storage systems (BESS) rival solar in incremental capacity. Using hourly CAISO data on prices, load, fuel costs, weather, and generator output from 2013-2024, I ask how massive storage deployment reshapes the classic "duck-curve" price dynamics. The regression compares the pre-storage period (Jan 2013-May 2017) with the recent high-storage period (2023-2024). Results show a structural break: whereas an additional 1 GWh of daily solar once raised evening real-time market (RTM) prices by about $0.4/MWh, the same shock in 2023-2024 reduces prices across all hours, including peaks.

Environmental Justice Effects of RGGI: Source-Level Emissions, Spillovers, and Exposure Inequality

with Xinghuan Luo

This project studies the environmental-justice implications of the Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative (RGGI), the first regional cap-and-trade program for U.S. power-sector carbon emissions. We estimate how RGGI affected CO2 and co-pollutant emissions from power plants, including potential spillovers to nearby non-RGGI PJM facilities. The analysis combines difference-in-differences estimates with a Poisson pseudo-maximum-likelihood counterfactual model, which predicts facility-level emissions in the absence of RGGI and recovers source-specific abatement for CO2, SO2, and NOx. Initial results show sizable reductions in CO2 and SO2 at RGGI facilities and heterogeneous abatement responses across coal plants. The next layer of the project would link these facility-level emissions changes to atmospheric transport and demographic exposure data, allowing the project to evaluate whether market-based climate policy reduced or shifted pollution burdens across communities.

Health Impacts of Coal Plant Retirements in the United States

with Xinghuan Luo

Coal plant retirements offer a real-world opportunity to measure how large reductions in industrial air pollution translate into public health gains. This project examines the health effects of U.S. coal-fired power plant retirements by linking plant-level retirement and emissions data with restricted-use mortality records and atmospheric pollution transport models. Instead of relying on fixed distance buffers around plants, the analysis uses InMAP source-receptor matrices to measure exposure based on actual pollution pathways across counties. The project also accounts for spillovers when counties are simultaneously affected by multiple active and retired plants. Using a two-stage difference-in-differences framework, the study compares health outcomes in areas exposed to retiring plants with cleaner control plants, aiming to produce more credible estimates of the public health benefits of coal plant retirement.

Algorithmic Collusion in Electricity Market Duopolies

This project studies whether independently trained reinforcement-learning agents can learn to tacitly collude in a stylised electricity market duopoly. Each firm submits a continuous linear supply function, observes only recent market-clearing prices and its own profits, and learns using Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient. I compare learned outcomes against competitive, supply-function Nash, Cournot, and monopoly benchmarks. The simulations show that cost structure is central: with linear costs, agents settle into destructive competition, while asymmetric quadratic costs make supply withholding privately attractive. Under quadratic costs, agents learn to set negative supply intercepts, restricting infra-marginal output and pushing prices near monopoly levels without communication. Patient agents sustain collusion longer than myopic agents, suggesting that adaptive pricing tools can generate supra-competitive outcomes from repeated market feedback alone.
